Medical AI, eye screening, 2026

Phone-based cataract screening on field images

Can a model score phone photographs of eyes for cataract, taken quickly in a busy charity hospital?

Through The Lens Project, I have worked at multiple eye camps with Vivekananda Mission Ashram Eye Hospital, Midnapur, over four years. There, a few ophthalmologists screen very large numbers of patients, and their examination time limits how many are seen. I am building a tool that scores the phone photographs a busy clinic actually takes (undilated eyes, hand-held, with flash, one attempt per eye), aiming to cut initial screening from 5 to 6 minutes to under a minute per patient. If it works, its major application is a self-screening kiosk in villages, for people who cannot spare a day for the hospital trip.

Problem and objective

The bottleneck in cataract screening at a high-volume charity hospital is the doctor's examination time, and delays send patients home unassessed. If a paramedic photographs each eye and a model scores it, patients reach the doctor already ordered by likelihood of cataract.

Paramedics work against a queue and take one photograph per eye, so any extra step, such as a retake, tends to be skipped. A tool is useful only if it accepts the image as taken.

Objective: a tool that works on these images as they are. This work measures how far a standard approach gets, finds what limits it, and identifies the path to remove that limit.

Clinical background

A cataract is a clouding of the eye's lens. Doctors distinguish three main types, and grade nuclear cataract from NS 1 to NS 4.

Implications for the model

  • Target NS 2 and above. NS 1 is rarely visible in a photograph, whatever the model.
  • Labels carry no grade, so early cataracts the photograph cannot show cap any score.
  • Doctors see through glare: cataract looks matte and yellowish, reflections crisp and pure white.
  • Screening is not a surgery decision. The tool orders or refers patients and never replaces a full eye examination.

Data

The images were collected on site at a remote charity eye hospital during routine screening, without any capture protocol. The treating ophthalmologist labelled 817 photographs in total: 298 cataract and 519 non-cataract, collected in three batches. Photographs showing both eyes were reviewed eye by eye with the ophthalmologist, so each eye has its own label. Both eyes of one photograph always stay in the same data split. After duplicate removal and quality control, the final dataset contains 744 eye crops.

  1. Collect817 photographs (298 cataract, 519 non-cataract), labelled by the ophthalmologist
  2. Label per eyeTwo-eye photographs reviewed eye by eye
  3. CleanDuplicates and non-eye crops removed
  4. Final set744 eye crops, 255 cataract, 489 non-cataract
  5. Split148 eyes locked as a test set, 596 for 5-fold cross-validation

Method

Pipeline

Duplicates removed by perceptual hashing, eyes found with Grounding DINO, 783 crops checked visually and 39 removed.

The upload was verified byte for byte. Duplicates were removed with perceptual hashing at photograph level (pHash, distance ≤ 5) and again at crop level. The detector gave 349 single-eye and 289 two-eye photographs. After clinical review, one crop per usable eye was cut (783), then checked visually on contact sheets, and 39 non-eye or glare-only crops were removed.

Cropping

Three versions, each driven by an observed failure of the previous one.

VersionMethodObserved failure
1Square crop at 1.4× the detected pupil boxDetector sometimes boxed a flash reflection as the pupil: crops of glare only
2Centred on the pupil, sized from the eye box (0.5 to 0.7× eye width)Remaining glare-only crops where the "eye" box was itself a reflection
3As 2, rejecting eye boxes under 8% of the image sideZoom still varies with gaze and distance. Accepted

Training and experiments

EfficientNet-B0, threshold set for 95% sensitivity. Four experiments, each changing one factor.

ImageNet-pretrained, one output, BCE loss, AdamW with a cosine schedule. Augmentation: random crop, flip, ±15° rotation, brightness and contrast ±20%. No hue change, because lens colour is a clinical cue.

ExperimentChangeQuestion tested
BaselineEye crops, 384 px, learning rate 2e-4, 20 epochsIs there a learnable signal?
Whole photoFull photographs instead of eye cropsDoes cropping lose information?
Slow learningLower learning rate (5e-5), 12 epochsIs training too aggressive?
Glare-spot removalSmall glare spots inpainted, 512 px, dropout 0.3 and weight decay 1e-2Do glare spots and overfitting limit it?

Results

  • Baseline AUC 0.692 (95% CI 0.648 to 0.734), 5-fold cross-validation on 596 eyes.
  • No change moved it: whole photos 0.57 to 0.77 per fold, slower learning 0.608, glare-spot removal 0.693.
  • At 95% sensitivity, specificity was only 0.14 to 0.18.
  • The model memorises: training loss fell from 2.19 to 0.05, while validation AUC peaked in the first epoch.

Performance plateaus at about 0.69. The locked test set is kept unused for a later model.

AUC 0.5 = chance. Sensitivity: cataract eyes flagged. Specificity: normal eyes cleared.

Why performance stops at 0.69

Lens opacity (light scattered inside the lens) and corneal reflection (light reflected at the cornea) both appear as whitish regions in the pupil. The ophthalmologist separates them reliably, and error analysis with the clinician gave the cues below.

The most reliable cue is spatial: whiteness continuing past the pupil edge is reflection. Brightness is not density, and reflection can sit on top of an opacity, so glare-spot removal based on brightness could not help.

CueLens opacityCorneal reflection
ExtentConfined to the pupilCan cross the pupil edge onto the iris
Inside vs outside the pupilDenser insideSimilar inside and outside
CharacterMatte, milkySpecular, crisp
Colour and edgeOff-white to yellowish, slightly blurredPure bright white, sharp

The limit is not the classifier but what it is able to see in the image.

The Roshni study from Uttar Pradesh reached 96% sensitivity and 97% specificity by checking blur and pupil quality at capture and asking for a retake when an image failed. In a heavy-volume hospital retakes are rarely practical, so this work takes the other route: a model that reads field images as they are.

Conclusion

Use now

Still useful for ordering the queue

At AUC 0.69 the model can already order the queue. Every patient is still examined and only the order changes, so patients more likely to have cataract reach the doctor sooner. Clinicians indicated that about 70% accuracy would already help here. It cannot be used to turn anyone away.

In a village kiosk, it could already act as an early nudge, prompting villagers whose photographs score high to visit the hospital, where some false positives are acceptable.

Major application

Self-screening kiosk in villages

A robust model serves both goals set out at the start: screening in under a minute per patient at hospital camps and, if it works, a self-screening kiosk for villagers who cannot spare a day for the hospital trip.

On field images as captured, an EfficientNet-B0 classifier (ImageNet-pretrained) reaches AUC 0.69, stable across changes to input, training and preprocessing. The limit is identified: under flash, glare and lens opacity look alike, and the model has not learned the boundary and density cues a clinician uses.

Because retakes are not practical in a high-volume clinic, the solution has to be a model robust to field images. This work is in progress and continues in this direction, starting with the glare label, pupil-edge cues and synthetic reflections.

Limitations

  • Small dataset. 255 cataract eyes is small for a subtle visual distinction.
  • One source. All images come from one hospital and one period.
  • Per-eye labels. In two-eye photographs the eye not named as cataract is labelled normal, and single-eye photographs keep their photograph label without per-eye review.
  • No grade. Labels carry no cataract grade, and early grades (NS 1) are rarely visible in photographs at all.
  • Possible batch bias. One batch contains only cataract images shared through a messaging app and is monitored for bias.
  • Test set unused. Results are cross-validated. The locked test set is unused by design.

Next steps: computer vision

The model must learn the error types of field images instead of having them filtered out.

A

Find the pupil edge precisely

Circular edge detectors (Hough, Daugman) refine the pupil outlines already found for all 744 crops.

B

Separate reflection from opacity

Specular and diffuse separation, colour and sharpness features, and a boundary-aware glare rule already tested on synthetic eyes.

C

Train on the error types

A second label for glare crossing the pupil edge, and synthetic edge-crossing reflections in training.

D

Check what the model looks at

Grad-CAM maps show whether it attends to the pupil interior, the reflection or the iris.

Each method will be compared with the current model on the same locked test set.
